### Description

- Titanium tetrachloride appears as a colorless fuming liquid with a pungent odor. Corrosive to metals and tissue. Very toxic by inhalation.[^1]
- COLOURLESS-TO-LIGHT-YELLOW LIQUID WITH PUNGENT ODOUR.[^2]

### Recommended use and restrictions

- **Sources/Uses:** Used to manufacture titanium-containing compounds; used as white pigments in paints; \[ATSDR ToxFAQs\] Used in chemical vapor deposition to apply coatings to metals and other materials with high melting points--the process involves bubbling hydrogen and nitrogen though a bath of titanium tetrachloride or [[Vanadium tetrachloride|vanadium tetrachloride]]. \[Scand J Work Environ Health 1991;17:369-79\] Used in dyeing textiles and leather (mordant), making glass, and in smoke screens; \[AIHA\][^3]
- **Industrial Processes with risk of exposure:** Painting (Pigments, Binders, and Biocides) \[Category: Paint\] Leather Tanning and Processing \[Category: Industry\] Textiles (Printing, Dyeing, or Finishing) \[Category: Industry\] Toxic Gas from Spilling Chemical in [[Water]] \[Category: Other\] Glass Manufacturing \[Category: Industry\][^3]
- Titanium tetrachloride is used as an intermediate in the production of titanium metal, titanium dioxide, and titanium pigments, in the manufacture of iridescent glass and artificial pearls, as a polymerization catalyst, and to produce smoke screens. Titanium tetrachloride was formerly used with potassium bitartrate as mordant in the textile industry and with dyewoods in dyeing leather.[^4]

## 5. Fire-Fighting Measures

### Explosion hazards

### Fire fighting

- Not flammable. For small fires, use dry chemical or carbon dioxide. For large fires, flood fire area with [[Water|water]] from a distance. Do not get solid streams of water on spilled material. ## 10. Stability and Reactivity

### Chemical stability

- EVOLVES DENSE WHITE FUMES[^9]
- CONCENTRATED AQ SOLN ARE STABLE[^9]

### Hazardous decomposition

- ... When exposed to moisture, it hydrolyzes to hydrogen chloride.[^9]
- When heated to decomposition it emits toxic fumes of /hydrogen chloride/.[^9]

### Corrosivity

- Corrosive[^9]

### Chemical dangers

- Decomposes on heating. This produces toxic fumes including hydrogen chloride. Reacts violently with [[Water|water]]. This produces heat and corrosive fumes including hydrogen chloride (see ICSC 0163). Contact with air generates [[Hydrochloric acid (32-38%)|hydrochloric acid]]. Attacks many metals in the presence of water.[^2]

### Air and water reactions

- Reacts exothermically with moisture in the air to evolve dense white fumes containing hydrochloric acid \[Merck 13th ed. 1996\]. Titanium tetrachloride reacts vigorously with water to generate gaseous HCl. Based on a scenario where the chemical is spilled into an excess of water (at least 5 fold excess of water), half of the maximum theoretical yield of Hydrogen Chloride gas will be created in 0.51 minutes. Experimental details are in the following: "Development of the Table of Initial Isolation and Protective Distances for the 2008 Emergency Response Guidebook", ANL/DIS-09-2, D.F. Brown, H.M. Hartmann, W.A. Freeman, and W.D. Haney, Argonne National Laboratory, Argonne, Illinois, June 2009.[^1]

### Reactive groups

- Acids, Strong Non-oxidizing[^1]

### Reactivity profile

- TITANIUM TETRACHLORIDE acts as an acid in aqueous solution. During the reduction of TITANIUM TETRACHLORIDE to titanium metal with potassium, an explosion occurred. The system had been heated to 90 °C \[Walter and Mandell 1967\]. Addition directly to tetrahydrofuran caused a violent exothermic reaction \[Inorg. Syn., 1982, 21, 135\]. Ethylene can polymerize at low pressure if catalyzed by titanium halides. (Sundaram, K. M, M. M. Shreehan, E. F. Olszewski. "Ethylene." Kirk-Othmer Encyclopedia of Chemical Technology. John Wiley & Sons, Inc. 2001.)[^1]

### Hazardous reactions and incompatibilities

- REACTS STRONGLY WITH WATER TO RELEASE HYDROCHLORIC ACID & HEAT.[^9]
- Addition of the anhydrous chloride directly to tetrahydrofuran causes a violent exothermic reaction.[^9]
- The liquid hexaurea complex formed during 6 weeks at 80 °C decomposed violently at above 90 °C.[^9]
- The 1:1 complexes with beryllium chloride or titanium tetrachloride may explode violently.[^9]
