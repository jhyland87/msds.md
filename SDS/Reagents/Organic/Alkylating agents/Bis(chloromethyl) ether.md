### Description

- Dichlorodimethyl ether, symmetrical appears as a colorless volatile liquid with a chloroform-like odor. Toxic by inhalation, skin absorption and ingestion. Dangerous fire risk - flash point below 0 °F. Vapors much denser than air. Insoluble in [[Water|water]] and denser than water. Used to make paints and varnish, and as a solvent.[^1]
- COLOURLESS LIQUID WITH PUNGENT ODOUR.[^2]

### Recommended use and restrictions

- **Sources/Uses:** Used in the past to make polymers, resins, and textiles; Currently, only small amounts are used in enclosed systems in the chemical industry. \[ACGIH\][^3]
- **Restricted Notes:** Regulated as a carcinogen; See 29CFR1910.1003;[^3]
- In the past, BCME was used for chloromethylation and as an alkylating agent in the manufacture of polymers. BCME is no longer used commercially in the United States. It is used as a research chemical and lab reagent.[^4]

### Fire hazards

- Container may explode in heat of fire. When heated to decomposition, it emits very toxic fumes of chlorides. Decomposed by water to [[Hydrochloric acid (32-38%)|hydrochloric acid]] and formaldehyde. Avoid water: hydrolyzes very rapidly (half life 10-40 seconds) on contact with water. Avoid decomposing heat, powerful oxidizers, areas of high fire hazard and moist air. (EPA, 1998)[^1]
- Highly flammable. Vapour/air mixtures are explosive.[^2]

### Hazards summary

- A corrosive substance that can cause pulmonary edema; \[ICSC\] Bis(chloromethyl) ether and [[Chloromethyl methyl ether]] (technical grade) are listed in Table 18-3 as substances that are known human carcinogens (IARC Group 1) after occupational exposure causing lung cancer (oat cell). \[Siemiatycki, p. 327\][^3]
- Bis(chloromethyl) ether is a clear liquid with a strong unpleasant odor. It does not occur naturally. It dissolves easily in water, but degrades rapidly and readily evaporates into air. In the past, it was used to make several types of polymers, resins, and textiles, but its use is now highly restricted. Only small quantities of bis(chloromethyl) ether are produced in the United States. The small quantities that are produced are only used in enclosed systems to make other chemicals. However, small quantities of bis(chloromethyl) ether may be formed as an impurity during the production of another chemical, chloromethyl methyl ether.[^14]
