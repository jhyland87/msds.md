### Description

- Diethyl sulfate appears as a clear colorless liquid with a peppermint odor. Burns, though may be difficult to ignite. Corrosive to metals and tissue. It is a potent alkylating agent. Flash point is 104 °C (219 °F) \[Aldrich MSDS\].[^1]
- OILY COLOURLESS LIQUID WITH CHARACTERISTIC ODOUR. TURNS BROWN ON EXPOSURE TO AIR.[^2]

### Recommended use and restrictions

- **Sources/Uses:** Used mainly to make dyes; Also used as an ethylating agent (pigment production), a finishing agent (textile manufacturing), a dye-set agent (carbonless paper), and an accelerator (sulfation of ethylene); Also used in agricultural chemicals, household products, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics; \[HSDB\][^3]
- **Industrial Processes with risk of exposure:** Pulp and Paper Processing \[Category: Industry\] Farming (Pesticides) \[Category: Industry\] Textiles (Printing, Dyeing, or Finishing) \[Category: Industry\][^3]
- Diethyl sulfate is primarily used as an ethylating agent, and also as an accelerator in the sulfation of ethylene and in some sulfonations. Diethyl sulfate is also a chemical intermediate for ethyl derivatives of phenols, amines, and thiols, and as an alkylating agent.[^4]

## 10. Stability and Reactivity

- Decomposes on heating. This produces flammable and toxic fumes. Reacts violently with ammonia, strong oxidants, strong bases and powdered metals. This generates fire hazard. Reacts with [[Water|water]] and moisture. This produces [[Sulfuric acid|sulfuric acid]] and ethanol.[^2]

- Combustible. Slowly reacts with water to form ethyl alcohol, a flammable liquid, and ethyl sulfate, with eventual conversion to sulfuric acid.[^1]

## 11. Toxicological Information

### Inhalation risk

- No indication can be given about the rate at which a harmful concentration of this substance in the air is reached.[^2]

### Short-term exposure

- The substance is corrosive to the eyes, skin and respiratory tract. Inhalation may cause lung oedema. The effects may be delayed. Medical observation is indicated.[^2]

### Long-term exposure

- This substance is probably carcinogenic to humans. May cause heritable genetic damage to human germ cells.[^2]

### Carcinogenicity

- Evaluation: There is inadequate evidence for the carcinogenicity in humans of diethyl sulfate. There is sufficient evidence for the carcinogenicity of diethyl sulfate in experimental animals. Diethyl sulfate is probably carcinogenic to humans (2A). In making the overall evaluation, the Working Group took into account diethyl sulfate is a strong direct alkylating agent which ethylates DNA and that as a result, it is genotoxic in virtually all test systems examined including potent effects in somatic and germ cells of mammals exposed in vivo.[^9]
- **IARC Carcinogenic Agent:** Diethyl sulfate[^15]
- **IARC Carcinogenic Classes:** Group 2A: Probably carcinogenic to humans[^15]
- **IARC Monographs:** Volume 54: (1992) Occupational Exposures to Mists and Vapours from Strong Inorganic Acids; and Other Industrial Chemicals Volume 71: (1999) Re-evaluation of Some Organic Chemicals, Hydrazine and Hydrogen Peroxide (Part 1, Part 2, Part 3)[^15]

### Routes of exposure

- The substance can be absorbed into the body by inhalation, by ingestion and through the skin.[^2]

### Signs and symptoms

- **Inhalation Exposure:** Cough. Sore throat. Laboured breathing. Shortness of breath. Symptoms may be delayed.[^2]
- **Skin Exposure:** Redness. Pain. Serious skin burns.[^2]
- **Eye Exposure:** Redness. Pain. Severe burns.[^2]

## 12. Ecological Information

*Non-mandatory for OSHA enforcement (29 CFR 1910.1200).*

### Environmental hazards

- The substance is harmful to aquatic organisms.[^2]

### Environmental fate summary

- Diethylsulfate's production and use as an ethylating agent for a wide variety of organic functional groups may result in its release to the environment through various waste streams. If released to air, a vapor pressure of 0.212 mm Hg at 25 °C indicates diethylsulfate will exist solely as a vapor in the ambient atmosphere. Vapor-phase diethylsulfate will be degraded in the atmosphere by reaction with photochemically-produced hydroxyl radicals; the half-life for this reaction in air is estimated to be 9 days.
